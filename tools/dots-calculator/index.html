DOTS calculator

A DOTS score is your total multiplied by 500, divided by a polynomial of your bodyweight in kilograms: DOTS = total × 500 ÷ (A·bw⁴ + B·bw³ + C·bw² + D·bw + E). A man weighing 80 kg with a 500 kg total scores 344.8. A woman weighing 60 kg with a 300 kg total scores 332.6.

Where DOTS comes from

DOTS stands for Dynamic Objective Team Scoring. The German federation BVDK introduced it so lifters of every weight class, and of both sexes, could score points for one team. Its author is Tim Konertz. The IPF itself uses its own GL points, while Wilks is the older formula many lifters still know. OpenPowerlifting reports DOTS for meet results.

DOTS for one lift

DOTS was built for a three-lift total, so a score for one lift isn’t comparable with competition totals. It is still a useful way to see whether you are getting stronger relative to your size. marble does exactly that: in Progress, each lift’s estimated 1RM is scored against the weigh-in nearest to it, and you choose men’s or women’s coefficients in Settings. Weigh-ins can come from Apple Health.

Questions

What is a DOTS score?

DOTS is a powerlifting score that adjusts a lifted total for bodyweight, so lifters of different sizes can be compared. It multiplies the total by 500 and divides by a fourth-degree polynomial of bodyweight in kilograms, with separate coefficients for men and women.

What does DOTS stand for?

Dynamic Objective Team Scoring. It was introduced by the German powerlifting federation BVDK, and its author is Tim Konertz.

How do I calculate DOTS in pounds?

Convert both numbers to kilograms first by dividing by 2.20462, then apply the formula. The calculator on this page does the conversion for you when you choose lb.

What is a good DOTS score?

There is no official tier list. Scores depend on federation, equipment and experience, so compare yourself with lifters in your own class on OpenPowerlifting, or with your own past scores.

Can I score a single lift with DOTS?

You can, but DOTS was designed for a three-lift total. A single-lift score is useful for tracking your own relative strength over time, not for comparing with competition totals. That is how marble uses it.

What happens at very light or very heavy bodyweights?

The polynomial is only fitted to competitive bodyweights, so bodyweight is clamped first: 40 to 210 kg for men and 40 to 150 kg for women. Outside that range the score uses the nearest limit.
